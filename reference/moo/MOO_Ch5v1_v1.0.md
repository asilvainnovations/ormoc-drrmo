# 1. Introduction and Assessment-informed Rationale

Chapter 4 ends when the activated EOC stands down. SOP 4-09 closes the EOC in a controlled manner, and its transition register (form ER-F05) lists what is still open: displaced families who cannot yet go home, damaged houses and lifelines, lost incomes, unresolved protection cases, and recovery recommendations. Chapter 5 receives that register. It sets out how the City turns the open items into an approved, funded and monitored program that restores living conditions and leaves Ormoc less exposed than before the event.

Recovery is the longest and most expensive phase of a disaster. It is also the phase in which the City has the most room to reduce future risk: houses can be rebuilt outside hazard zones, bridges can be rebuilt to a higher standard, and livelihoods can be diversified. The Philippine DRRM system names this phase "rehabilitation and recovery", one of its four thematic areas, and sets "Build Back Better" as its guiding principle (NEDA, 2020). Build Back Better means that reconstruction restores pre-disaster conditions and also raises resilience standards and includes climate adaptation and mitigation measures.

Ormoc knows the cost of recovery from experience. The 1991 flash flood (Typhoon Uring) remains the deadliest event in the City's record. Super Typhoon Yolanda (2013), the magnitude 6.5 Leyte earthquake (2017), Typhoon Ursula (2019) and Typhoon Odette (2021) each left damage that took months or years to repair (Table 5.3). The City has carried out a PDNA before (the 2017 earthquake PDNA cited in the DRRM 10-Year Plan) and runs a cash-for-work program after disasters. What it lacks is a standing system: a pre-disaster recovery framework, a trained PDNA team, a decision route that is written down, and a way to track recovery projects to completion.

This chapter applies the same systems-thinking and Total Quality Management (TQM) approach as Chapters 1 to 4. Recovery is treated as a cycle with feedback: plan before the event; assess after it; program, fund and build; track to completion; evaluate; and feed the lessons into the land use plan, the contingency plans and the LDRRMP. A program document is not evidence of recovery. Recovery is complete only when the planned outputs are delivered, the intended outcomes are checked, and the residual risk is lower than before.

# 2. Purpose and Intended Results

This chapter sets the City's minimum arrangements for planning recovery before a disaster, assessing damage, losses and needs after it, preparing and approving a rehabilitation and recovery program, delivering it to Build Back Better standards, and tracking it to completion. It does not create new legal powers. Decisions on plans, budgets, land use, relocation and procurement stay with the authorities named in law and in valid City issuances.

The intended institutional results are:

-   A pre-disaster recovery framework, adopted by the CDRRMC and updated each year, that names roles, PDNA activation, temporary shelter, debris management and recovery fund sources before an event (A9.5).

-   A designated and trained RDANA and PDNA capability, with a written decision route from rapid assessment to PDNA and to CDRRMC approval (A8.7, A9.1).

-   A controlled handover from response to recovery in which every open item from the Chapter 4 transition register has a named recovery owner (SOP 4-09 to SOP 5-03).

-   A rehabilitation and recovery program, prepared after each major event, approved through the proper route and containing build-back-better and resettlement provisions (A9.2).

-   Build-back-better screening of recovery projects before funding, linked to the land use plan, the zoning ordinance and the Building Official's assessments (C1.4, C2.1).

-   One referral pathway for psychosocial, social protection, livelihood, agriculture and shelter services, with consent and privacy controls (A9.4).

-   A recovery monitoring matrix that tracks damage, losses, livelihood and housing outputs to completion and reports them to the CDRRMC (A9.3).

-   Transparent recovery communication with a feedback and grievance channel for affected people.

-   Outcome evaluation and after-action learning that revise plans and standards (A10.6, Chapter 7).

# 3. Scope, Application and Institutional Boundaries

This chapter applies to recovery from any disaster that affects the City, whatever the hazard: tropical cyclones, floods and flash floods, landslides, earthquakes, tsunami, fire and industrial or geothermal incidents. It applies to the CDRRMC and its proposed Rehabilitation and Recovery Committee, the CDRRMO, the City offices that deliver recovery services, the barangays and their BDRRMCs, and the partners that work through City coordination arrangements. It covers pre-disaster recovery planning, damage and needs assessment, program formulation and approval, delivery, monitoring, evaluation and closeout.

The lead council depends on the area affected. The City leads when the disaster is confined to Ormoc and affects two or more barangays; a BDRRMC handles a disaster confined to one barangay. Because Ormoc is an independent component city, when the same disaster affects Ormoc and the Province of Leyte or another independent city, the RDRRMC VIII leads the overall rehabilitation and recovery coordination (NEDA, 2020). In that case the City still prepares its own local program, which feeds the regional program.

# 6. Pre-Disaster Recovery Planning

Most of the work that makes recovery fast is done before the disaster. The NEDA guide lists six pre-disaster activities for local DRRMCs: build the recovery database, prepare science-based scenarios, identify critical areas and populations, set up the recovery organisation, list PPAs that can be scaled up or reprogrammed, and arrange recovery financing (NEDA, 2020). This section assigns each one to an owner. SOP 5-01 turns them into an annual cycle, and Annex 5-C gives the outline of the resulting framework document.

## 6.1 Recovery Baseline Database

The CDRRMO, as CDRRMC secretariat, keeps the recovery baseline. It reuses the Chapter 3 risk-information base and adds the data that recovery planning needs: population disaggregated by sex, age and disability at barangay level; housing and settlement data; livelihoods, farms and fisheries; MSMEs; critical facilities and lifelines; and social services. Annex 5-E lists the minimum data, adapted from Annex A of the NEDA guide. The barangay unit is the PSGC barangay (85 barangays as of 31 July 2025), pending the master-list reconciliation recorded in Chapter 3.

-   Keep the baseline as aggregates by barangay. Household lists stay with the office that owns them (for example, CBMS with CPDO and beneficiary lists with CSWDO).

-   Record the source and date of every dataset. A blank value means "not reported", never zero.

-   Update the baseline at least once a year as part of SOP 5-01, and after every event that triggers a PDNA.

## 6.2 Recovery Scenarios

Recovery scenarios describe what a disaster of a given size would do to people, buildings, lifelines and livelihoods. They are taken from the CDRA and the contingency-plan scenarios of Chapter 3, so that the same most-probable and most-severe scenarios drive preparedness, response and recovery (C1.1). For each priority hazard the framework records the likely number of damaged houses, interrupted lifelines, affected farms and businesses, and the recovery needs these imply. Scenarios are prepared by the City with technical support from PHIVOLCS, PAGASA, MGB and NAMRIA; local and indigenous knowledge may be used.

## 6.3 Critical Areas, Assets and Populations

From the scenarios the CDRRMO and CPDO identify, before any event, the areas, assets and population groups whose recovery comes first: lifeline roads and bridges named in the CDRA, water and power facilities, the port, hospitals, schools used as evacuation centres, settlements in high-hazard zones, and barangays with high poverty incidence. The list sets the order of the PDNA field work and of the first PPAs.

## 8.3 Phasing of PPAs

Short-term PPAs, implemented within a year, meet basic needs and restore social services so that families can return to normal life. Medium-term PPAs, implemented over the following three to four years, continue those programs and add measures that reduce future risk. Some reconstruction may run longer. In the 10-Year Plan, livelihood, social services and agriculture outcomes are targeted in the short term, while housing and infrastructure need phasing for studies, land acquisition and social preparation (City Government of Ormoc, 2023).

## 8.4 Approval and Updating

The CDRRMC prepares and endorses the program; the Sangguniang Panlungsod approves it and the related appropriations. When funding from the national government is requested, the CDRRMC submits the Sanggunian-approved program through the RDRRMC VIII to the NDRRMC, and the RDRRMC reviews and validates the City PDNA (NEDA, 2020). The program includes an investment program that is revised as implementation progresses, based on the physical and financial accomplishment reports of SOP 5-07.

# 9. Build Back Better

Build Back Better is applied as a decision rule, not a slogan. Every recovery project that rebuilds or repairs a structure, a lifeline, a settlement or an ecosystem is screened before it is funded (SOP 5-05, form REC-F07). The screen asks whether the project reduces risk compared with what was there before, and it records the decision and its reasons.

## 9.1 What Build Back Better Does Not Authorise

This Manual does not declare no-build zones, change land use classifications, order relocation or set design thresholds. Those require an ordinance, a valid executive issuance, a building permit decision or a technical standard issued by the competent body. The screen in SOP 5-05 records whether such an instrument exists and, if it does not, refers the issue to the authority that can issue it. One such instrument already exists: City Ordinance No. 37, s. 1993 declares parts of the Alegria–Punta shoreline and the Anilao and Malbasag river banks as high-risk areas where habitation, construction and building permits are prohibited (Chapter 1, section 4.5). Its boundaries must be reconciled with current hazard maps before the screen relies on it.

## 9.2 Post-disaster Land Use Framework

For a disaster with major physical impact, the CPDO prepares a land use framework for the program that identifies where infrastructure, settlements and economic activities will be rebuilt, based on the CLUP, the Zoning Ordinance, the shelter plan and updated risk assessments. Where the disaster changes the landscape (for example, new landslide scars or river course changes), the framework proposes the changes needed in the CLUP or Zoning Ordinance for Sanggunian action. Where there is no significant physical change, the framework is used to enforce the existing land use policies (NEDA, 2020).

## 9.3 Resettlement Safeguards

-   Relocation is a last resort, after in-place rebuilding with risk reduction has been considered and found unsafe.

-   Relocation follows RA 7279 and the applicable DHSUD rules, including consultation, adequate notice and due process.

-   Resettlement sites include livelihood, social services, infrastructure and utilities, and are themselves screened for hazard.

-   Affected families, including women, older persons and persons with disability, take part in site selection and design.

-   The City records each decision, its legal basis and the consultation held; individual household records stay with the housing office and CSWDO.

# 11. Inclusion, Protection and Community Participation

Recovery is hardest for those who were most vulnerable before the disaster: displaced families; orphaned, unaccompanied and separated children; older persons; persons with disability; single-parent households; indigenous peoples; and farmers, fisherfolk, farm workers and small traders who lose their income (NEDA, 2020). The program shall:

-   Set priority beneficiaries using pre-disaster vulnerability, capacity to recover and access to services, and record the criteria publicly.

-   Use sex-, age- and disability-disaggregated aggregates and gender-sensitive indicators in the monitoring matrix; the Harmonized Gender and Development Guidelines may be used to review PPAs.

-   Respect culture, beliefs and cultural sites, and take account of the needs of indigenous peoples in restoring their assets.

-   Consult affected communities, including women, youth, older persons and persons with disability, during assessment, planning, implementation and monitoring.

-   Provide a feedback and grievance channel that is accessible and that does not depend on literacy or a mobile phone (SOP 5-08).

-   Protect people from exploitation and abuse in the delivery of assistance, and refer protection cases to CSWDO.

# 13. Recovery Monitoring, Tracking to Completion and Evaluation

## 13.1 Monitoring Unit

The CDRRMC designates the unit responsible for monitoring the program before implementation starts, so that monitoring is not left out (NEDA, 2020). This chapter proposes the CDRRMO Research and Planning function, working with the CPDO monitoring staff, as the recovery monitoring unit. Its responsibilities are to set key performance indicators; orient implementing offices on the monitoring tool; consolidate their reports; keep the project database up to date; take up implementation issues with the offices concerned; prepare periodic status reports to the CDRRMC; and validate reports through field visits to key projects. If a task force is created for a large event, the transfer of the monitoring function back to the permanent unit is agreed at the start.

## 13.2 Recovery Monitoring Matrix

All recovery PPAs, whatever their fund source or implementing office, are tracked in one recovery monitoring matrix (form REC-F06). This closes the A9.3 gap and is the recommended action for that indicator ("add a recovery monitoring matrix to the event database"). Annex 5-D lists the minimum fields.

## 13.3 Reporting and Problem Solving

-   Each implementing office names a monitoring focal person and reports physical and financial accomplishment against targets at the frequency set by the CDRRMC. The NEDA guide requires at least an annual report; this chapter proposes monthly reports during the first year and quarterly reports afterwards, for CDRRMC decision.

-   Issues that block implementation are raised as they arise, not only in the formal report.

-   A regular inter-office problem-solving meeting, with the monitoring unit as secretariat, resolves bottlenecks.

-   The monitoring unit and office focal persons validate key and problem projects in the field.

## 13.4 Data Protection in Recovery Records

Beneficiary lists, household damage records and case files are personal or sensitive personal information under RA 10173. They are held by the office that serves the person, with access limited to staff who need them. The monitoring matrix, MODE and public reports carry aggregates by barangay and by PPA only. Small counts that could identify a person are referred to the City Data Protection Officer before publication.

## 13.5 Outcome Evaluation and Learning

For a large event the CDRRMC commissions an outcome evaluation of the whole program or selected sectors, to check whether the target outcomes were achieved and whether the PPAs contributed to them. Projects are selected using criteria such as number of beneficiaries, contribution to local income, jobs generated and basic services provided. The evaluation is participatory and supervised by the monitoring unit even when a third party conducts it (NEDA, 2020). Its findings, together with the AAR from Chapter 4 (ER-F06), go to the Chapter 7 corrective-action register and revise the pre-disaster recovery framework.

## 13.6 MODE Platform Support

MODE does not yet hold a recovery module. Forms ER-F05 and ER-F06 from Chapter 4 are loaded in the SOP registry, and the Data Dictionary lists recovery entities (recovery assessments, plans, actions, service referrals and frameworks) for functional area 10 (MR-11). Once this chapter is seeded, its SOPs are carried as Library drafts and as workflows or protocols, and forms REC-F01 to REC-F09 are configured as step forms. The monitoring matrix can be kept in a controlled workbook until a MODE screen is built. Seeded content stays a draft until the owning office validates it and the CDRRMO Head approves it.

# 14. Standard Operating Procedures

The following procedures set the minimum control sequence for recovery. Before adoption, the CDRRMO shall reconcile them with the current OCD PDNA Guidance Notes, the City's approval routes and the IAT v1.7 Action Plan (Annex 5-A). Each procedure shall be loaded in MODE as a workflow (a multi-office management cycle) or a protocol (a point-of-action checklist) and tested through a tabletop exercise or, for SOP 5-02, a PDNA simulation.

Figure 5.6 places the eight procedures in the recovery phases. Each procedure has a control block (type, the proposed MODE template, the legal decision authority kept apart from the operational owner, trigger, forms, indicators and policy basis) and a step table. Every step states its actor, action, decision or criteria, inputs, outputs and records, timing, control and escalation.

# 20. Conclusion

Chapter 5 continues the operational chain of Chapter 4 past the closing of the EOC. It makes recovery a planned system: the City prepares before the event, receives the response handover item by item, assesses damage and needs through a written decision route, prepares and approves a program that builds back better, delivers services through the offices that hold the mandates, and tracks every project to completion. Its central control is traceability: each recovery action can be traced from the PDNA need to the approved PPA, the BBB screen, the fund source, the output on the ground and the lesson learned.

Writing these procedures does not implement them. Institutionalisation requires an adopted pre-disaster recovery framework, a designated and trained PDNA team, a decision on the recovery committee, a signed referral agreement, a working monitoring matrix and at least one exercise or real event in which the procedures were used and reviewed. Until then, this chapter remains a controlled working draft.
