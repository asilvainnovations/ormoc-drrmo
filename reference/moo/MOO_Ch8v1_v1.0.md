# 1. Introduction and Assessment-informed Rationale

No city manages disaster risk alone. In Ormoc, the decisions that most change risk are made outside the CDRRMO: where the CPDO allows development, whether the Building Official has checked an evacuation centre, how fast LEYECO V restores power to the hospital, whether the schools can keep teaching, whether a neighbouring town can lend a rescue team when every Ormoc team is deployed. Chapters 1 to 7 set what the CDRRMO and the EOC do. This chapter sets how they work with everyone else, before, during and after an event.

The chapter covers two kinds of relationship. The first is **external**: with the Province, the region and national agencies; with neighbouring LGUs; and with CSOs, volunteer groups, the private sector, utilities, academe, faith-based groups and communities (Figure 8.1). The second is the **interface** with other City offices that own systems on which disaster risk depends. The IAT measures this second kind separately, in Part C, and this chapter is its home in the Manual.

## 1.2 Design Considerations

1.  **Mandates stay with their owners.** Agreements set who does what and how to ask; they never move command, legal powers or accountability.

2.  **Agree before the event.** A partner who has signed, been briefed and been tested in a drill can be called in minutes; a partner who has not is a hope.

3.  **Technical, reciprocal and apolitical.** Mutual aid is framed as an exchange between technical offices for defined resources, activated by request and governed by the host LGU's command, so that it survives changes in leadership.

4.  **One register.** Every agreement, its term, its focal person and its review date sit in one partner register, so that lapsed or unsigned agreements are seen and not counted.

5.  **Interfaces are owned elsewhere.** For Part C, the lead office owns the indicator and the work; the CDRRMO coordinates, supplies risk information and reports. This chapter sets the interface, not the technical content, which stays in Chapters 3 to 6.

6.  **Inclusion and accountability.** Groups that represent women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities and other at-risk people are partners, not only beneficiaries, and communities have a way to raise concerns.

This chapter sets how the City identifies, agrees, authorises, registers, exercises, activates and reviews its DRRM relationships with other governments, organisations and City offices.

3.  Each Part C lead office has an interface agreement naming its focal person, the actions it owns and how it reports, and the Interface Index rises from its baseline (MR-17).

4.  Requests for augmentation follow the vertical route of the national response system, and incoming teams are received, briefed, tasked and demobilised in order.

5.  Power, water and telecom providers have standing restoration-priority arrangements (C2.2).

6.  The CDRRMC has its full membership, with SP-accredited CSOs, all private-sector seats filled and an academe seat (A1.2).

7.  Partners meet at least once a year before the typhoon season, and their commitments are recorded in the Chapter 6 resource inventory (B6.6).

8.  Assistance to other LGUs follows a lawful, documented route.

# 5. Systems Thinking and Partnership Principles

Partnerships fail in predictable ways. A partner is called only during events, so neither side knows the other's capacity; agreements are signed by one administration and forgotten by the next; the same few organisations are asked every time while others drift away. These are feedback problems: engagement that happens only under pressure builds little trust, and low trust makes the next request slower. The remedy is steady engagement between events, so that trust and readiness accumulate.

-   **Relationships before resources.** Meet partners before the season, exercise with them, and review with them after events. The annual partner forum (SOP 8-07) is the main mechanism.

-   **Reciprocity.** Mutual aid that only flows one way is charity; agreements state what each side can offer and how costs are settled.

-   **Institutional, not personal.** Agreements name positions and offices, not persons, and survive changes in leadership.

-   **Clear interfaces.** Every interface names who leads, who supports and who decides, so that partners do not wait for each other.

-   **Accountability to affected people.** Partners working with affected communities follow the same feedback and protection rules as City offices (Chapter 5, SOP 5-08).

# 6. Interface Governance with City Offices (Part C)

## 6.1 Principle

Part C of the IAT measures systems that reduce or manage disaster risk but sit outside the CDRRMO: land use and investment planning, building safety, lifelines, ecosystems, sector anticipatory actions and business continuity. Each indicator names a lead office. The lead office owns the indicator, the work and the evidence. The CDRRMO coordinates, supplies hazard and risk information, convenes the annual interface review and reports the Interface Index to the CDRRMC. The Interface Index is never added to the IMI, so a weak interface cannot be hidden by a strong CDRRMO score, or the reverse.

## 6.3 Interface Agreements and the Annual Review

Each lead office signs an interface agreement with the CDRRMO (PTN-F04): a joint office order or memorandum of understanding between City offices, approved by the City Administrator, or included in an executive order where the Mayor prefers. It names the focal person by position, the Part C indicators the office owns, the actions it will take in the year, the information the CDRRMO will give it, and how it reports. Once a year, before the IAT cycle, the CDRRMO convenes the lead offices for an interface review (SOP 8-04): each office presents its evidence, the Interface Index is computed, and gaps go to the CAPA register (Chapter 7). In MODE, the indicator_lead_offices table links each Part C indicator to its lead office, so the lead office's focal person sees and scores its own items.

**Requesting augmentation.** When City capacity is or will be exceeded, the EOC prepares a request that states the need, quantity, location, time needed and the City's own resources already committed. The Mayor, as CDRRMC Chair, sends it to the Province, and through the Province to the region where national assets are needed, as the National Disaster Response Plan and the regional SOPs provide (SOP 8-03). The RA 12287 request for a State of Imminent Disaster is the one exception that goes directly from the CDRRMC to the RDRRMC VIII, under Chapter 1, SOP GOV-05. Incoming national or provincial teams are received at a staging area, briefed and tasked through the EOC, and work under the Incident Commander.

# 8. Horizontal Coordination: Mutual Aid With Other LGUs

Ormoc's neighbours (Kananga, Albuera, Merida and Isabel) share its watersheds, roads and hazards. A flash flood in the Anilao and Malbasag catchment, an earthquake on the Philippine Fault or a geothermal incident can affect several of them at once, or overwhelm Ormoc while its neighbours are spared. RA 7160 Sec. 33 allows LGUs to coordinate efforts, services and resources by agreements approved by their sanggunians. The IAT recommends **technical mutual aid agreements**, framed between DRRM offices for defined resources, so that cooperation does not depend on political alignment.

**Table 8.9**

*Elements of a Technical Mutual Aid Agreement*

| **Element**                     | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
|---------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Parties and authority**       | The LGUs, each acting through its Mayor with prior sanggunian authority; the Province may join.                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Scope**                       | Defined resource types: search and rescue teams, emergency medical teams, heavy equipment, water tankers, communications, evacuation centre capacity, technical staff (engineers, damage assessors, CCCM staff); information sharing on shared hazards. |
| **Activation**                  | By written or recorded request from the requesting LGU's Mayor or authorised DRRM officer; the assisting LGU may decline if its own area is threatened.                                                                                                 |
| **Command and safety**          | Resources work under the host LGU's Incident Commander under ICS, keep their own supervisors, and may be withdrawn by the sending LGU for its own emergency.                                                                                            |
| **Duration and demobilisation** | Default period per deployment, renewable; demobilisation through the host EOC with a handover record.                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Costs**                       | Who bears travel, fuel, food and lodging; how any reimbursement is settled; consumables replaced in kind or paid.                                                                                                                                       |
| **Liability and insurance**     | Each LGU insures its own personnel and equipment; treatment of injury, damage and third-party claims.                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Information**                 | Shared contact directory, warning and SitRep exchange; data protection under Chapter 7.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Exercise and review**         | At least one joint exercise or call-down test a year; annual review; term and renewal.                                                                                                                                                                  |

*Note.* Annex 8-B gives a model outline. Terms are to be negotiated by the parties and reviewed by the City Legal Office; this chapter fixes none of them.

Watersheds that extend beyond the City (C3.2) are a natural first subject: the Anilao–Malbasag and Pagsangaan catchments link upstream land use to downstream flood risk, and an agreement on information sharing and joint monitoring with DENR, EDC and the upstream LGUs can be negotiated alongside mutual aid. The 10-Year Plan's planned Leyte DRRMCs Telecom and Response Network is the route for joining a provincial network.

# 10. Civil Society, Volunteers, Faith-based and Humanitarian Organisations

-   **CSOs on the CDRRMC.** The five CSO seats are filled by organisations accredited by the SP; their representatives bring community knowledge and reach. SOP 8-06 sets how seats are filled and renewed.

-   **Volunteer groups.** Organised groups such as the CPR-RIMT Rapid Rescue Riders work under signed MOAs that state training, equipment, tasking, insurance and liability. Individual community disaster volunteers are accredited under RA 10121 Sec. 13 (Res. No. 07, s. 2024) and the City volunteer program (Ordinance No. 160, s. 2024); Chapter 9 trains them. Spontaneous volunteers in an event are registered and tasked through the EOC, never deployed unregistered.

-   **Philippine Red Cross.** As an auxiliary to the government in the humanitarian field (RA 10072), the Red Cross sits on the CDRRMC and the CCCM Team; its roles in first aid, welfare, blood services and restoring family links are agreed in advance.

-   **Faith-based groups.** Churches, mosques and other faith communities often shelter evacuees and distribute relief. No agreement with any faith-based group was found. The CDRRMO invites them to the annual partner forum and agrees, where they wish, on the use of their facilities as evacuation sites (subject to the Building Official's safety check) and on relief coordination.

-   **CCCM partners.** The City CCCM Team (EO of 2022) brings partners into camp management under the JMC No. 02, s. 2021 standards; their roles are part of the partner register.

-   **Sectoral groups.** Organisations of persons with disabilities, older persons, women, children and youth are invited to planning, exercises and reviews, so that plans reflect their needs (with the PDAO, OSCA and GAD focal point).

# 11. Academe, Science and Development Partners

The City's academic and scientific partners give it research, data, training and technical review it could not afford alone: the tertiary institutions under the Education Resilience and DRRM Coordination Group (CDRRMC Res. No. 04, s. 2025), the City College of Ormoc and EVSU Ormoc, the Oscar M. Lopez Center (SP Res. No. 2021-357), the National Resilience Council and EDC through Project TRANSFORM. Their work is recorded in the partner register with its outputs. Research that uses City data follows a data-sharing agreement (Chapter 7, SOP 7-09). Development partners and donors are engaged through the same register; funds and goods they provide follow Chapter 6.

# 12. Communities and Accountability to Affected People

Barangays are the City's closest partners. Their BDRRMCs plan, warn, evacuate and report; Chapter 9 builds their capacity and Chapter 4 coordinates them in events. This chapter adds two commitments. First, communities have a way to raise concerns and suggestions about DRRM services at any time, through the CDRRMO hotline and feedback desk, and partners working in communities use the same channel. Second, the City reports back: the annual results report (Chapter 7) is published, and the CDRRMO explains to barangays what changed after major events.

# 20. Concluding Notes

Ormoc already has more partners than many cities: national agencies, utilities, a geothermal operator and five CSOs on its Council; a resilience partnership with the NRC and EDC; volunteers under agreement; tertiary institutions in a coordination group; and a record of helping its neighbours. What is missing is the standing arrangement that makes each relationship dependable on the worst night: a signed mutual aid agreement, a restoration-priority list, an interface agreement with each City office that owns part of the risk, and a register that shows which commitments are real.

Writing these procedures does not create the partnerships. Until the partner register holds signed agreements, at least one mutual aid agreement is in force and has been exercised, the Part C lead offices have signed interface agreements, and the first partner forum has been held, this chapter remains a controlled working draft.
