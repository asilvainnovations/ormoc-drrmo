## 1. Introduction and Assessment-Informed Rationale

A warning works only if people understand it and know what to do. A contingency plan works only if the people named in it have practised it. A barangay can account for its residents within hours only if its BDRRMC is organised, trained and has done it before. This chapter is about those people: the public, barangay officials and volunteers, school and hospital staff, City office personnel and the EOC roster. It sets how the City builds their awareness and their skills, and how it keeps those skills from fading.

Chapter 2 develops the CDRRMO's own staff (competency framework, training needs analysis and SOP HR-04). Chapter 4 issues warnings and notices during events. This chapter covers capacity beyond the CDRRMO and between events: public awareness, the City's annual DRRM training and exercise programme, barangay and community resilience, volunteers, and the readiness of schools, hospitals and evacuation centres.

The baseline shows activity without measurement (Figure 9.1). Domain A7 (Preparedness and Community Capacity) has an index of **2.12 (Developing)**, with 2 of 5 Mandatory items compliant. Training, drills and IEC happen, but most scores rest on the FGD alone and fell to 2 under the evidence rule: the training calendar, drill evaluations, IEC reach and BDRRMC functionality are all still to be shown. Evacuation centres do not yet meet the Ligtas Pinoy Centers standards (A7.4, High). There is no refresher cycle (A7.8), no skills inventory across offices and barangays (A2.10), and no record of a barangay accounting for its residents within a set time (A7.9) (Table 9.1).

### 1.2 Design Considerations

1.  **Competence, not attendance.** A training counts when the person can do the task, shown in an exercise or real event. Attendance sheets are records, not proof.

2.  **Capacity drains.** Staff move, barangay officials change after each election, and unused skills fade. Every capacity has a refresher date (Figure 9.2).

3.  **Practise in order.** Drills, then tabletop exercises, then functional and full-scale exercises; each evaluated, each finding tracked.

4.  **Barangay first.** The first responders in Ormoc are its 85 BDRRMCs and their puroks. Support goes first to the barangays that are least functional and most exposed.

5.  **Reach, not activity.** IEC is measured by the people and households reached and what they know or do, not by the number of seminars held.

6.  **Inclusive by design.** Messages, trainings, drills and evacuation centres are designed with women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities and other at-risk groups, not adjusted afterwards.

## 2. Purpose and Intended Results

This chapter sets how the City builds and sustains awareness and capacity for disaster risk reduction and management across its people, communities and institutions.

Intended results:

1.  An annual City DRRM training programme, based on a needs analysis of City offices, BDRRMCs, schools, responders and volunteers, is approved and delivered, with evidence of competence (A7.1).

2.  Every trained role has a refresher date, and attendance and lapse are tracked (A7.8); a skills inventory across offices and barangays is updated every 12 months (A2.10).

3.  An annual exercise programme, from NSED drills to an EOC functional exercise and a full-scale exercise, is run and evaluated (A7.2, B7.3).

4.  An IEC programme with annual reach targets per barangay is delivered in Ormocanon (Bisaya) and accessible formats (A6.4, A6.6), under an approved risk communication plan (A6.5).

5.  All 85 BDRRMCs are assessed for functionality each year, low-functioning barangays receive a support package (A7.3), and each barangay can account for residents of affected puroks within 12 hours (A7.9).

6.  The volunteer programme has a registry, accreditation, insurance, training and deployment rules (A2.8).

7.  Evacuation centres are audited against national standards and improved in priority order (A7.4); schools and hospitals take part in planning and drills (A7.7).

8.  Sector representatives confirm that they were engaged in plans, trainings and drills (A7.6).

## 5. Systems Thinking: Capacity as a Stock

Capacity behaves like water in a tank with a leak (Figure 9.2). Training fills it; staff turnover, barangay elections and unused skills drain it. A City that trains intensively once and then stops will see its capacity fall back within a few years, however good the training was. Two levers matter more than the size of any single course: a refresher cycle and regular exercises, which slow the drain, and a skills inventory, which shows how full the tank is.

- **Plan for the outflow.** After each barangay election, the CDRRMO runs an orientation for new BDRRMC members early in their term.

- **Trainers multiply capacity.** A local pool of trainers (CDP PPA 2.6.2), drawn from the CDRRMO, BHERTs, volunteers and partners, lets the City reach 85 barangays without waiting for outside courses.

- **Exercises are where capacity is proven.** Each evaluated exercise is evidence for the IAT at levels 3 and 4.

## 6. Public Awareness and Risk Communication

### 6.1 The IEC Programme

The IEC programme sets, for each year, the messages, audiences, channels and reach targets per barangay (A6.6). Messages follow the hazards and plans of Chapter 3: what each warning means, where the evacuation centre is, what to bring, how to check on neighbours, and what a State of Imminent Disaster is and what anticipatory actions it may bring. Reach is counted as people or households reached, by barangay, sex and age group where possible, and a short knowledge check is used in a sample of barangays each year.

### 6.2 Local Language and Accessible Formats

Messages are written in Ormocanon (Bisaya), with English where needed, and tested with residents before use (A6.4). Each core message has accessible versions: Filipino Sign Language video (RA 11106), large-print and pictorial versions, plain-language audio, and visual alerts for Deaf and hard-of-hearing residents. Where a message relays an official PAGASA or PHIVOLCS product, the translation keeps its meaning and the original wording is kept on file (Chapter 4).

### 6.3 Risk Communication Plan and Public Information Officer

The City designates a DRRM public information officer and an alternate, and adopts a risk communication plan (A6.5) that sets spokespersons, approval of messages, coordination with the City Information Office, media relations, monitoring of rumours and misinformation, and correction. Between events the plan governs the IEC programme; during events Chapter 4 governs what is issued and by whom.

### 6.4 Preparing the Public for Anticipatory Action

RA 12287 adds a new message: the City may declare a State of Imminent Disaster and ask people to act days before impact. The IEC programme explains this before any declaration, so that the public notices issued under Chapter 4 (A6.7) are understood when they come. Geographically isolated barangays are reached through their BDRRMCs and VHF radio, not only by mobile networks.

## 7. The City DRRM Training Programme

### 7.1 Needs Analysis and Annual Plan

Each year the CDRRMO, with the HRMO, analyses training needs across City offices, the EOC roster, BDRRMCs and BHERTs, schools, responders and volunteers, using the competency matrix (Annex 9-B), the skills inventory, IAT gaps and AAR findings (SOP 9-01). The analysis for CDRRMO staff is done under Chapter 2 (HR-04) and folded in. The result is one annual City DRRM training plan with courses, target groups, providers (CDRRMO trainers, OCD VIII, DOH, DSWD, BFP, Red Cross, academic partners), schedule, cost and evaluation method, funded through the LDRRMFIP (Chapter 6).

### 7.2 Delivery, Records and Evidence of Competence

Training records state the course, provider, dates, participant (by position and office, or barangay), competency addressed, assessment result and refresher date (CAP-F03). Records of City employees are also sent to the HRMO. Competence is shown by assessment at the end of the course and by performance in an exercise or event. MODE does not yet hold training records; until it does, CAP-F03 is kept as a controlled register.

### 7.4 Skills Inventory

Every 12 months the CDRRMO updates an inventory of DRRM-relevant skills across the CDRRMO, CDRRMC member offices, BDRRMCs, BHERTs and accredited volunteers: search and rescue, first aid, engineering, logistics, GIS, damage assessment, PDNA, MHPSS, communications, and languages including Filipino Sign Language (A2.10). ## 8. Drills and Exercises

The City exercises its plans in a progression (Figure 9.3). Drills test one task (evacuation of a school, a siren test, a call-down). Tabletop exercises test plans and decisions around a scenario. Functional exercises activate the EOC against a simulated event without deploying field teams. Full-scale exercises bring the EOC, field teams and partners together. Each is planned with objectives, evaluated under Chapter 7 (SOP 7-04) and followed by tracked actions.

## 9. Barangay and Community Resilience

### 9.1 BDRRMC Functionality and Support

Every year the CDRRMO, with the DILG City Office, assesses the functionality of all 85 BDRRMCs (A7.3), using the DILG barangay preparedness assessment (Listo si KAP, as cited in IAT v1.7) and the City's functionality matrix (CAP-F06): organisation, plan reviewed (Chapter 3), early warning, evacuation, BHERT, equipment, drills held and reporting. Barangays are grouped into three support tiers (Figure 9.4). Low-functioning barangays and those with the highest exposure in the CDRA receive a priority package; higher-functioning barangays become peer mentors.

### 9.2 Accounting for Residents Within 12 Hours

After an event, each affected barangay should be able to say, within 12 hours of impact, how many of its residents are safe, injured, missing or displaced, purok by purok (A7.9). The BDRRMC does this through purok leaders using the household lists the barangay already keeps, and reports counts, never names, to the EOC. The welfare check is practised in the hazard-specific drill and timed (CAP-F07). Named lists stay with the barangay and the CSWDO (Chapter 7, SOP 7-09).

### 9.3 Barangay Health Emergency Response Teams

Ordinance No. 171, s. 2024 provides for BHERTs in all barangays. Their training and refreshers are part of the City training plan, with the City Health Office, and their readiness is part of the BDRRMC functionality assessment.

## 10. Volunteers

The City volunteer programme (Ordinance No. 160, s. 2024) and the accreditation of community disaster volunteers (Res. No. 07, s. 2024) give the City a reserve of trained people. Chapter 2 (section 14.3) sets the HR rules: volunteers supplement and never replace required positions, and their roles, supervision, safety, insurance and limits are documented. This chapter runs the programme (SOP 9-07): recruitment, accreditation, registry, training, insurance under the risk categorisation adopted in 2022, and deployment rules. During activation, volunteers are tasked through the EOC or the Incident Commander, not directly by the organisations that recruited them. Accredited volunteers may be given anticipatory action tasks under RA 12287 IRR Rule IV Sec. 5, such as helping with pre-emptive evacuation and checking on at-risk households. Spontaneous volunteers in an event are registered at a single point and tasked, or politely redirected.

## 11. Evacuation Centres, Schools, Hospitals and Inclusion

### 11.1 Evacuation Centre Standards

Evacuation centres are audited against RA 12076, DSWD standards and the NDRRMC Disaster Preparedness Minimum Standards (SOP 9-08; Annex 9-D): location in a low-risk area; structural safety (the Building Official's assessment under Chapter 8, C2.1); WASH facilities separated by sex; privacy and family spaces; ground-floor priority and access for persons with disabilities, older persons and pregnant women; child-friendly spaces; a health and first-aid area; security; kitchen and storage; and power and communications. Retrofits are prioritised for centres serving the 46 critical flood barangays and those rated "Poor" in the CDRA, and funded through the LDRRMFIP (Chapter 6). Schools are used as evacuation centres only as a last resort and for the shortest time, consistent with RA 10821.

### 11.2 Schools and Hospitals

DepEd SDO schools, the tertiary institutions in the Education Resilience and DRRM Coordination Group, and the City's hospitals and health facilities take part in contingency plan tabletop exercises and in drills each year (A7.7). Their own preparedness plans remain theirs; the CDRRMO aligns triggers (class suspension under Ordinance Nos. 135, s. 2023 and 189, s. 2025; hospital surge) and communications with them. Education and health anticipatory actions are Part C items owned by DepEd and the CHO (Chapter 8).

### 11.3 Inclusive DRRM

Women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities, indigenous peoples and other at-risk groups take part in designing messages, trainings, drills and evacuation centre improvements, through the IPGDO, the PDAO, the OSCA and their organisations. Once a year, a short consultation with sector representatives asks whether they were engaged and what should change, and their answers are recorded as evidence for A7.6 (CAP-F05). Data on participation and reach are disaggregated by sex, age and disability where this can be done without identifying anyone.

## 12. MODE Support

MODE holds the EOC roster and designations (Chapter 2), the protocols run in drills (each run is a record), barangay reports and resource requests, and the Library and Vault where training materials and exercise reports are kept. It does not yet hold training records, the skills inventory, the BDRRMC functionality matrix or the volunteer registry. Until it does, these are kept on CAP forms as controlled registers; section 16 lists the platform additions. Any future table holding volunteer or trainee personal data follows Chapter 7 (SOP 7-09): positions and counts in reports, names only where the City as employer or programme owner needs them, with access limited by role.

## 19. Concluding Notes

The City already trains, drills and informs, and it has put BHERTs, volunteers and school partnerships into ordinance and resolution. What it lacks is the evidence that these activities produce capacity, and the rhythm that keeps capacity from draining away: a needs analysis, a refresher date for every skill, a progression of exercises, a functionality check for every barangay, a reach target for every message, and evacuation centres that meet the standard.

Writing these procedures does not build capacity. Until the first combined training plan has been delivered with competence records, the first EOC functional exercise has been evaluated, all 85 BDRRMCs have been assessed and the evacuation centre audit has begun, this chapter remains a controlled working draft.
